# Lower Palisades Google Drive Document Summaries

This document summarizes the fourteen project-related items returned by the Google Drive search for "lower palisades." Together, they describe the restoration proposal, its supporting research and outreach, the local policy context, and the history of development and preservation along the Palisades.

## 1. Lower Palisades Restoration Project - Jersey City Environmental Commission - June 16, 2026

This presentation introduces the Lower Palisades Restoration Project to the Jersey City Environmental Commission. It defines the Lower Palisades as the portion of the Palisades south of the George Washington Bridge, extending through dense communities in Bergen and Hudson Counties. While the better-known northern cliffs benefit from the Palisades Interstate Park Commission and other conservation organizations, the southern corridor has little coordinated environmental management. The presentation estimates that approximately 400 acres of natural land remain across the corridor, including about 100 acres in Jersey City.

The presentation connects this landscape to the full geological and cultural history of the Palisades. The cliffs originated as a diabase sill roughly 200 million years ago and were later exposed and shaped by glacial activity. It recounts the Lenape name associated with the cliffs and the conservation campaign that stopped destructive quarrying around the beginning of the twentieth century. That campaign created the Palisades Interstate Park, but its protections and active stewardship largely stop north of the project area.

The Lower Palisades still provide valuable urban tree canopy and habitat. Photographs and observations document deer, foxes, salamanders, bats, snakes, birds, and native vegetation living in this fragmented corridor. The presentation identifies invasive plants, improper vegetation management, and accumulated trash as the most urgent problems. Tree of heaven, kudzu, Japanese knotweed, English ivy, and Norway maple are among the principal invasive species. Existing maintenance often consists of repeatedly topping vegetation to preserve views rather than removing invasives and restoring a stable native plant community.

The proposed response is to begin with city-owned land below Riverview Park. This pilot would combine difficult-terrain trash removal, professional invasive-species remediation, native planting, outreach to people living in cliffside encampments, and sustained maintenance. The presentation recommends developing a funded master plan, partnering with specialized organizations, and learning from restoration work at places such as Highbridge Park. It also argues that the Palisades Preservation Overlay District and proposed state legislation should protect the ecology of the cliffs, not only their skyline and scenic appearance.

## 2. Notes/ideas - Lower Palisades Restoration Project

This is the project's main working research document. It describes the Palisades as a geologically and ecologically important formation running along the Hudson River and identifies a management gap south of the George Washington Bridge. The notes estimate approximately 400 acres of land of interest across Bergen and Hudson Counties, including roughly 130 acres in Jersey City, and organize the project around five goals: removing invasive plants, cleaning trash, coordinating homeless outreach, improving public access, and building community participation.

The document is also a directory of possible partners and resources. It lists invasive-species experts, restoration organizations, native-plant resources, sanitation and technical-climbing groups, homeless-service providers, environmental nonprofits, accessibility advocates, government contacts, tax maps, land records, legislation, and grant programs. Potential restoration techniques include using goats to suppress kudzu, solarizing infestations, replacing invasives with native meadow or woodland vegetation, and requiring the owner of the former Christ Hospital property to address its large kudzu patch.

The notes preserve a local environmental history that may otherwise be lost. They document the topping of trees below Riverview Park in July 2021, followed by perceived declines in bats and increases in mosquitoes; campfires associated with encampments during the winter of 2024-2025; planting work undertaken between 2021 and 2025; cliff land cleared for an unrealized development; and wildlife observations including deer, salamanders, snakes, foxes, raptors, and migratory birds. They also collect old images, rescue incidents, examples of cliff cleanup in other cities, and information about the Christ Hospital redevelopment.

A detailed species list identifies trees, shrubs, herbs, grasses, ferns, mosses, and lichens associated with North Jersey rock outcroppings. This provides an ecological reference for future planting and restoration plans. Overall, the document functions as a living project notebook: part resource catalog, part field log, part historical archive, and part list of prospective interventions.

## 3. IN THE REGION: New Jersey; Limiting Growth on the Lower Palisades

This 1988 New York Times article reports on the Palisades Conservation Plan developed by the Regional Plan Association and the Trust for Public Land. At the time, high land prices and strong demand for housing and waterfront construction threatened the remaining natural landscape. Seventeen projects representing more than 2,000 homes were already proposed or underway, while state projections anticipated more than 15,000 residences and 12 million square feet of commercial space at the base of the cliffs.

The plan covered approximately 1,000 acres and fifteen miles from Fort Lee to Jersey City. It recommended steep-slope ordinances, height limits, setbacks, and protections for views both toward and from the cliffs. It also proposed a noncontiguous greenway, acquisition of 65 acres for twelve parks, and rehabilitation of 52 trails, stairways, and walkways connecting communities to the cliffs and waterfront.

Because the Palisades cross ten municipalities, the plan argued that voluntary local action would not be enough. It proposed state legislation requiring municipal zoning to align with regional goals, coordination by the state environmental agency, and an extension of the Palisades Interstate Park Commission's jurisdiction to the lower cliffs. Local officials generally supported preservation but worried about acquisition costs and state control. Developers questioned whether they or municipalities would be compensated for lost development rights.

The article demonstrates that the present restoration project continues an unresolved regional-planning effort nearly four decades old. Its central tension remains current: individual municipalities and landowners make decisions parcel by parcel, while the cliffs operate as a single ecological, scenic, and geological system.

## 4. Lower Palisades Restoration Project - June 16, 2026 - NOTES

These speaker notes provide the full narrative behind the Environmental Commission presentation. Jason Biegel explains that his proposal concerns a neglected urban habitat rather than the protected hiking landscape most people associate with the Palisades. He frames restoration as a climate-resilience, public-health, and quality-of-life project capable of reducing heat and flooding, improving air quality, supporting wildlife, and connecting residents with nearby nature.

The notes establish his relevant experience: horticulture and urban-forestry education, farm work, service with local environmental and tree organizations, urban-agriculture teaching, participation in a regional heat-mapping study, workshops on safe raised-bed gardening, construction of artificial nesting habitat for least terns, and operation of a tree-watering alert system. This background explains how long-term observation of the cliffs developed into a broader stewardship proposal.

The historical portion contrasts the successful conservation of the northern Palisades with the neglect and destruction still visible south of the George Washington Bridge. The notes use old and current images below Riverview Park to show that the land has become more wooded over time but still lacks intentional ecological management. They describe native wildlife and plants as evidence that the corridor retains substantial habitat value despite its degraded condition.

The core problems are dense invasive vegetation, destructive maintenance practices, trash, and encampments in terrain conventional sanitation crews cannot safely reach. The notes use the 2021 tree topping below Riverview Park as an example of an expensive cycle: invasive trees are cut without being eradicated, then regrow and require the same treatment. A better approach would remove the invasive plants and replace them with lower-growing native species that preserve views without repeated damage.

The proposed pilot below Riverview Park would begin on an existing city-owned park parcel. Specialized service organizations would handle outreach to unhoused residents, technical crews would remove trash from steep slopes, and invasive-species professionals would perform ecological restoration. A master plan would define initial work, recurring maintenance, schedules, budgets, funding sources, and stakeholder roles. The notes conclude with a request for the Environmental Commission to help guide the effort and recruit additional advocates.

## 6. PPOD AMENDMENT meeting - July 30, 2026

These meeting notes document a community discussion about amending Jersey City's Palisades Preservation Overlay District, or PPOD. Participants opposed redevelopment that would cantilever buildings into the cliff and argued that the ordinance's definitions, triggers, and enforcement mechanisms are too ambiguous. Earlier cases had exposed weaknesses in determining whether a property contained a protected cliff face, especially where historic fill or dumping concealed the original geology.

Proposed changes included clearer geological definitions, licensed geological or geotechnical analysis, restrictions on soil stripping and vegetation removal, stronger drainage and erosion standards, and rules requiring intrusive existing footprints to be pulled back during major redevelopment. Participants emphasized that protecting views alone is insufficient: stormwater failures, slope instability, and vegetation loss can threaten buildings, infrastructure, and the cliff itself.

The discussion repeatedly linked land-use regulation with the restoration project. Jason Biegel described beginning with trash removal and invasive control below Riverview Park and highlighted the urgency of containing kudzu around the former Christ Hospital. Participants also discussed unclear ownership and maintenance responsibility for difficult cliff parcels, including land adjacent to Sergeant Anthony Park. Even where the city owns land, agencies have sometimes avoided maintenance because the terrain is challenging.

The notes assign concrete follow-up work. Sophia Pereira would evaluate proposed PPOD edits, research precedents from other cities, consult planning and sustainability staff, clarify definitions, and recommend revisions. Other participants would arrange site visits, consult additional experts, gather storm observations, and open a separate planning discussion about the medical district around Christ Hospital. The meeting therefore connects three related needs: stronger development rules, better enforcement of stormwater requirements, and active ecological stewardship of public land.

## 7. The Palisades: Beauty and the Beast

Paul Goldberger's 1976 New York Times article examines the high-rise transformation of the Palisades opposite Manhattan. He describes the result as a chaotic collection of buildings produced without a coherent relationship to the cliffs, neighboring structures, roads, or public space. By contrast, he points to Riverside Drive as an example of tall residential development organized around a consistent landscape and urban plan.

The article explains how developers marketed the New Jersey cliffs to New Yorkers as both close to the city and safely removed from its problems. That appeal accelerated apartment construction, but the architecture ranged from respectable to extremely poor. Goldberger complicates a purely preservationist account by noting that the lower cliffs had already been altered by factories, warehouses, and deteriorated commercial uses; the pristine landscape many critics imagined existed primarily farther north.

The underlying planning problem was political fragmentation. High-rises appeared in several municipalities, each with different zoning rules, and some with almost no effective controls. Decisions made independently by Fort Lee, Cliffside Park, Edgewater, North Bergen, Guttenberg, West New York, and other communities produced a shared skyline with no shared design. The article provides an early diagnosis of the regional-governance problem later conservation proposals attempted to solve.

## 8. Jersey City Birds writeup

This short outreach document introduces the restoration project to potential birding and nature partners. It describes the forested cliffs as one of Hudson County's largest remaining natural areas and as important tree canopy and wildlife habitat. Invasive plants, illegal dumping, and the absence of coordinated stewardship threaten those benefits.

The project would begin on the city-owned parcel below Riverview Park, using cleanups adapted to steep terrain, invasive-species remediation, native planting, and community participation as a pilot for broader work. The document identifies public awareness as a central challenge and proposes partnering with birdwatching organizations to help residents recognize the ecological value of the cliffs.

The outreach message connects habitat protection with direct community benefits: cleaner air, reduced urban heat, improved mental health, and greater biodiversity. Birding is therefore presented not as a separate recreational activity but as an accessible way to build the constituency needed for long-term restoration.

## 9. Groundworks Grant Application

This application requests funding for a short documentary about the Lower Palisades Restoration Project. It summarizes the condition of the cliffs, identifies the Riverview Park parcel as the pilot site, and proposes cleanup, invasive-species control, native planting, partnerships, and community engagement. The project is modeled partly on successful urban-forest restoration by the New York Restoration Project in Highbridge Park.

The proposed film would explain the ecological importance of the cliffs and the specialized techniques required to restore steep urban terrain. Distribution through schools, community events, social media, and partner organizations would attract volunteers, funders, and institutional support. The application cites prior outreach, including an Environmental Commission presentation, birdwatching events, and Councilmember Jake Ephros's endorsement.

Groundwork Grants declined the request. The response praised the restoration vision but explained that a documentary was primarily a marketing and awareness activity, while the funder's priorities favored direct community work. The reviewers encouraged a future application focused on restoration itself. This feedback suggests that future proposals should request funds for tangible field work—such as cleanup, invasive removal, planting, equipment, or volunteer events—with communications treated as a supporting expense.

## 10. NEW JERSEY OPINION: LET'S GUIDE DEVELOPMENT TO SAVE THE PALISADES

James L. Wunsch's 1985 New York Times opinion essay argues that waterfront redevelopment creates both a threat and an opportunity for the Lower Palisades. It contrasts the protected cliffs within Palisades Interstate Park with the neglected southern cliffs, which were defaced by signs, graffiti, weeds, dumped appliances, vehicles, and poorly placed development.

As obsolete shipping and industrial uses gave way to residential, commercial, and recreational waterfront projects, Wunsch argued that the cliffs should be treated as the waterfront's monumental natural backdrop. Buildings should preserve views of rock and foliage, remain below or visually subordinate to the ridge, and avoid cutting into the cliff face. Properly designed development could gain value from the landscape rather than destroy it.

The essay calls for consistent standards across municipal boundaries. Local height restrictions often protected views from the top of the Palisades while allowing large buildings below to obscure views of the cliffs themselves. Without coordinated design controls, Wunsch warned, cumulative development could make the rocky wall disappear from public view. The piece is an important precedent because it explicitly extends preservation beyond the northern park and frames the Lower Palisades as a regional landscape requiring collective action.

## 11. Opinion: LG's Plan to Deface the Palisades

This 2014 New York Times editorial opposes LG Electronics' proposal for a 143-foot headquarters in Englewood Cliffs. The planned tower would rise well above the tree line in an area where a 35-foot limit had historically protected the natural profile of the Palisades. Local officials changed zoning rules to permit the design, raising concerns that one exception would invite additional towers.

The editorial emphasizes the breadth of opposition. Four former New Jersey governors, New York officials, federal agencies, the Palisades Interstate Park Commission, the National Park Service, preservation organizations, and environmental advocates asked LG to reduce the building's height. The Environmental Protection Agency withdrew its earlier approval after recognizing the project's visual impact.

Because LG controlled a large site, the editors argued that it could satisfy its space needs with a lower design. They portrayed the company's refusal as corporate insensitivity and warned that a tower permanently visible above the cliffs would become a symbol of disregard for a nationally important landscape. The piece illustrates how municipal zoning exceptions can undermine preservation agreements with regional significance.

## 12. Opinion: Don't Spoil the Palisades

This 2013 New York Times editorial addresses the same LG headquarters proposal at an earlier stage. It recalls the century-old New York-New Jersey effort to place the Palisades beyond destructive quarrying and development, then argues that a tower four times the usual height limit would violate that legacy.

The proposed building would be visible from Fort Tryon Park and other locations across the Hudson. Four former New Jersey governors warned that it would interrupt a historic natural vista and establish a precedent for taller buildings extending north along the cliffs. The Environmental Protection Agency revoked its support, with its regional administrator comparing the visual intrusion to placing an office tower on the rim of the Grand Canyon.

The editorial concludes that the site's size made a substantial low-rise alternative feasible. Redesigning the headquarters would protect history, the environment, and LG's public reputation without preventing the company from building.

## 13. Jake Ephros letter of support

This letter records formal support from Jersey City Ward D Councilmember Jake Ephros for the Lower Palisades Restoration Project and project manager Jason Biegel. It recognizes the cliffs south of the George Washington Bridge as an important urban natural area that provides tree canopy, cleaner air, cooling, and ecological value in a densely developed part of the city.

The letter endorses a practical first phase on the city-owned parcel below Riverview Park, identified as Block 3003, Lot 1. It describes that pilot as the beginning of a broader management strategy addressing invasive species, illegal dumping, habitat restoration, and sustained stewardship.

Ephros also positions the project as a useful extension of existing policy. The Palisades Preservation Overlay District and proposed Palisades Cliffs Protection and Planning Act primarily protect the physical and visual character of the cliffs; the restoration project adds active ecological management. The letter is intended to strengthen funding applications by demonstrating municipal support.

## 14. Quietly, Land on the Palisades Goes on Sale

This 1978 New York Times article reports on the proposed sale of Rio Vista, a 300-acre forested estate in Alpine and Cresskill, for $10 million. The parcel was believed to be the largest undeveloped tract of its kind near New York City and one of the last major development opportunities on the northern Palisades' western slope.

The muted reaction contrasted with the decade-long campaign that had recently preserved a comparable 294-acre tract in Tenafly. Conservation organizations and the Palisades Interstate Park Commission had fewer funds because of inflation and the cost of earlier acquisitions. Before offering Rio Vista to developers, the owner gave Alpine an opportunity to buy its portion at a reduced price with potential Green Acres assistance, but residents rejected the purchase in a close referendum.

The article places the sale within the broader history and geography of the Palisades. It recounts the formation and historical importance of the cliffs and describes the long campaign against quarrying that led to the interstate park. It also notes significant differences between affluent northern communities and the denser municipalities below the George Washington Bridge. The episode shows that preservation depends not only on public concern but also on financing, local votes, land values, and the willingness to acquire property before development occurs.

## Overall themes

The documents consistently identify the Lower Palisades as a regional natural system managed through fragmented local ownership and regulation. Development controls have historically concentrated on height, views, and cliff stability, while active habitat management has received much less attention. The restoration project fills that gap by connecting land-use protection with invasive-species control, trash removal, native planting, wildlife habitat, public access, and recurring stewardship.

The strongest near-term strategy reflected across the collection is to begin with a visible, achievable pilot on city-owned land below Riverview Park. Direct field work would establish credibility, generate evidence for costs and methods, and respond to the funding feedback already received. At the same time, PPOD amendments and regional partnerships could address the structural issues that a single pilot cannot solve: inconsistent zoning, unclear agency responsibility, stormwater and erosion risks, private ownership, and the absence of a corridor-wide management institution.
